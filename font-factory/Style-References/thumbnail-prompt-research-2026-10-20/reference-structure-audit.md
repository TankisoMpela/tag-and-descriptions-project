# 33-Reference Thumbnail Composition Audit

Reviewed again against all 33 downloaded reference images in `references/` and their complete descriptions in `captions.json`. The captions retain the visible subject, crop, surface, lighting, focus, texture, typography and secondary copy in full. This audit extracts the design mechanics that can transfer to new font thumbnails. It does not turn each reference into a checklist or an imitation target. The descriptions remain observational: some source images physically print or mount type on objects, but that treatment is excluded from this seller's thumbnails.

The seller's constraints override details that happen to appear in source examples. In particular, decorative stars, sparkles, badges, speckles, gratuitous glow, clutter, and excessive microdetail are not recommendations.

## Composition patterns confirmed across the set

1. **The title owns the first read.** It is often the largest shape, spans most of the canvas, or is the only sharp/high-contrast item. A smaller image can establish mood, movement or product context, but it must not outshine the name.
2. **The title stays a flat graphic layer.** The source set shows titles over photographs, on graphic fields, and in illustrations. Use those relationships: keep type crisp, front-facing, and independent of background perspective and lighting. Physical writing or dimensional lettering that is attached to a 3D scene is out of scope even when it appears in a reference.
3. **Human imagery is frequently cropped or demoted.** Several examples remove eyes/faces, show only a lower face or torso, or make a person a distant silhouette. For this workflow, models are background atmosphere: crop above the eyes, soften the face, reduce its contrast/exposure, and put the crisp type in front. Never make facial beauty the main visual payoff.
4. **Useful blur has a job.** Haze and shallow focus flatten a forest, valley, clothing or a person into a calm field; motion blur belongs to moving secondary figures. Keep lettering sharp. Don't add blur uniformly or use it as a generic filter.
5. **A named material earns its texture.** Sweater knit, marker ink, aged paper, halftone print, pixel grid, glass reflections and film haze are legible because each comes from a specific surface or medium. Pick at most one dominant material treatment; omit unrelated grain, specks and effects.
6. **Supporting images are specific and few.** One fruit, character, athlete, palm, glass, or silhouette can make the font idea concrete. The item either crosses, frames, backs or balances the title. Extra labels, decorative badges and tiny copy are common in the source set but not needed for this user's thumbnails.
7. **Type-only references still have an authored structure.** Giant cropped glyphs, size contrast, a strong color division, or one graphic mark keeps the layout designed. A centered name on an empty field is not a complete thumbnail for this workflow.
8. **Color must protect the title locally.** Cream on dark photo, yellow on darkened action, black on pale sky and dark type on coral are clear because light/dark separation persists behind the letters. Avoid similar-value colors behind the glyphs, including two dark colors or two near-neighbor tints of one hue. A mixed scene needs a stable clean patch, an intentional solid backing plane, or enough luminance contrast wherever the text crosses.

## Prompt-making sequence

- Inspect the actual white-background font-name specimen. Record exact casing and two or three visible construction traits; don't substitute a broad label like “script” for what the letters do.
- Form one visual thesis: “Because these letters have **[observable trait]**, show **[one subject/setting/graphic action]** using **[one reference-backed composition mechanism].” If the image would work just as well for an unrelated font, make the idea more specific.
- Pick a structure from this audit based on the font and buyer context: typographic field with deliberate scale/crop, title across a cropped photo, one illustrative subject, subdued atmospheric scene, or restrained print treatment. Do not rotate through these mechanically.
- Keep the name as a flat front-facing 2D title over the scene or within a 2D graphic. Never describe lettering printed, painted, mounted, embossed, extruded, or perspective-warped onto an object, garment, page, wall, product, or 3D environment.
- Choose enough detail to make the layout and premise legible, but do not enforce a word count. Describe frame structure, title scale/placement, subject, palette and focus; do not repeat every detail in the reference caption or turn it into a decorative inventory.
- State what stays sharp, what recedes, and the foreground/background value relationship. For a person, keep the face out, cropped, softly blurred or subdued so the type leads.
- Review the output at full size and thumbnail size. Remove elements not tied to the premise; correct typography, clipping, weak composition, face emphasis, surface-embedded lettering and contrast before accepting it.
