# Thumbnail Reference Findings and Art Rules

## Evidence reviewed

All 33 saved thumbnail images and their complete, individual captions in `captions.json` were reviewed again while correcting the prototype set. The captions preserve the visible subject, crop, typography, exposure, focus, surface, texture, and copy. `reference-structure-audit.md` records the transferable structure of each image. Captions are evidence about what the examples contain; they are not a shopping list of details to reproduce.

## What makes the references feel designed

- **The name is the visual event.** It is often two large lines, fills most of the frame, crosses a strong image, or is the sharpest and highest-contrast shape. The references do not merely place a font name on a blank canvas.
- **Composition comes from relationships.** The name pushes against a crop, crosses a photo, occupies a quiet patch in a busy scene, balances one illustration, or plays against a second scale of the same letterforms. A setting or object explains the idea; it does not become a second hero.
- **The frame has structure.** Strong examples use asymmetric placement, tight cropping, a large image counterweight, a clear color division, an intentional title band, or oversized glyph fragments. These choices make even a restrained poster feel complete.
- **The supporting image has a reason to be there.** A peach, runner, penguin, glass, palm, silhouette, or landscape gives the title a setting or a useful visual counterweight. One specific subject works better than a pile of props.
- **Photo and illustration use different kinds of depth.** Photographic backgrounds can have focus falloff, haze, perspective, or motion. Illustrated references use a small shape vocabulary and deliberate flat layers. The title remains a crisp, flat graphic layer in front of either kind of background.
- **Color carries hierarchy.** The strongest titles separate from their local background in value and hue: ivory on a dark scene, black on a pale field, or a bright distinct accent on a subdued photo. A pleasing palette alone does not make the name readable.
- **Texture belongs to the medium.** Knit, dry marker, paper grain, halftone, pixel edges, glass reflections, and film haze work because the reference has a reason for them. Use one relevant treatment and leave unrelated effects out.
- **Restraint is not emptiness.** A type-only image still needs an authored layout: purposeful scale contrast, cropped glyphs, a strong asymmetry, a framed color field, or a single meaningful graphic. A centered name on a nearly blank field is a raw specimen, not a finished thumbnail direction.
- **The reference set is varied.** It moves among still life, close photo crop, cinematic landscape, action photography, print poster, flat character illustration, and typographic composition. Repeating one layout with new colors is not real variety.
- **Variation must change the idea, not just the color.** Before a new font prompt, compare the recent thumbnail concepts by medium, composition, focal subject/device, palette family, texture/light, and title placement. Keep the name dominant and make the next concept differ across several of those choices.
- **Do not use “premium” as a default concept.** Glossy tabletop still lifes, amber window light, velvet or silk, perfume/flower props, shallow-focus product scenes, and gold accents can make unrelated fonts look like the same generated image. Use a photo only when its subject has a clear, font-specific reason; a clean typographic, illustrated, or other purposeful scene can be stronger.
- **Restrained does not mean textureless.** A flat layout can use one fitting material cue—paper tooth, uneven ink, a print grain, or a faint illustration—to feel authored. Keep it quiet and specific to the concept; scattered flecks, particles, and stacked effects make the image noisy rather than tactile.

Do not assume that photo, illustration, or a plain field is inherently right or wrong.

The shared problem is that generic geometry became the idea. A new flat design still needs a font-specific visual reason: a meaningful illustration, a material or print treatment, a subject, or a deliberately composed type relationship. Do not replace those rejected layouts with the same poster in a new palette. These examples are exclusions, not a ban on geometry when a particular font genuinely calls for it.

## Seller-specific hard rules

1. **Keep every title flat and front-facing.** The supplied name is a 2D graphic overlay in front of the scene or a flat 2D poster composition. It must never be written, printed, painted, mounted, embossed, extruded, perspective-warped, or embedded onto a photographed or rendered 3D object or environment. A photo or 3D scene may be the background; the title stays independent of its surfaces, lighting, shadows, and perspective. No sign lettering, lettering on clothing or fabric, or words placed on a photographed page or product.
2. Start from the supplied white-background specimen. Preserve its exact spelling, natural capitalization, contours, joins, spacing, and filled/outlined construction. Only use all caps when the specimen itself is all caps.
3. Make the font name the first read, followed by one concise, accurate style descriptor in title case. Use the same descriptor on the 3:2 main thumbnail and square main thumbnail, secondary but readable at small size. Keep important glyphs fully visible. Do not add unrelated copy or unsupported product claims.
4. Build a real thumbnail composition, not a specimen on an empty backdrop. Pair the name with one clear structure: a cropped photo, one meaningful subject or illustration, a strong split/color field, layered type scales, or a distinctive framing shape. For type-only artwork, use at least two deliberate layout moves beyond “large centered words on a flat color” (for example, scale contrast plus cropping, or asymmetry plus a related geometric device).
5. Choose one visual premise. Every supporting object, shape, or background treatment must make that premise clearer or give the title a needed counterweight. Do not decorate unused areas to make the design feel busy.
6. For photographs, decide what is sharp, what recedes, and where the title sits. People remain secondary: crop out the eyes/face, show only a lower face or body, or keep the face soft and low contrast. Motion blur belongs only on secondary moving forms.
7. Check contrast behind every glyph. Avoid dark type on dark imagery, two near-value colors, or a title whose readability changes as it crosses the frame. Use a distinct light/dark relationship or a clean overlay field when needed.
8. Never add glitter, stars, sparkles, starbursts, confetti, floating particles, fake lens flares, arbitrary badges, generic AI ornaments, gratuitous glow, random 3D geometry, or texture stacks. Do not add extra swashes, scratches, labels, or tiny copy unless the font or user brief specifically requires them.
9. Do not assign a visual recipe by category (script = flower, serif = portrait, display = sports). The actual glyphs, name, and useful buyer context must earn the concept.
10. Do not enforce a prompt word count. Give enough detail to describe frame structure, title scale and placement, background subject, palette, and focus, then stop. Add composition detail, not extra props.

## Required design pass before rendering

### Novelty pass

Use the five most recently updated, non-archived font plans in
`font-factory/fonts/` as the comparison set. Compare each plan's 3:2 thumbnail
concept and existing `visual-direction/candidate-review.md` route note; inspect
a final image at small size only when one exists. Do not mistake the white
specimen placeholder for a finished thumbnail. If fewer than five plans exist,
use the current prototype gallery and its `review.md`. Privately sketch three
genuinely different directions, then choose the one that best expresses the
actual specimen and buyer context. Compare medium, composition, focal
subject/device, palette, texture/light, and title placement. Avoid repeating
three or more of those dimensions from recent work, and do not repeat the same
dominant photo trope in consecutive fonts. Do not print private reasoning; add
one short novelty note to the already-required `candidate-review.md`, naming
the selected mechanism and what recent pattern it changes. If a repeated motif
is essential to the font, say why in that same sentence and vary the other
dimensions.

Write a compact composition map before the image prompt:

- What visible letterform trait is being showcased?
- What specific subject, scene, or graphic structure belongs with that trait or name?
- Where does the title sit, how much of the frame does it occupy, and what does it overlap?
- Which color values keep every letter readable?
- What one or two supporting elements make this feel like a finished thumbnail rather than a raw specimen?
- Would one restrained texture or faint illustration give the composition a more tactile, authored finish?
- Which nearby concepts or layouts should this avoid repeating?

Then check the render at full size and at 300×200. Do not accept an image solely because it followed the prompt. Reject it if the layout looks like an empty title sample, if a supporting detail competes with the name, if the concept could belong to any font unchanged, or if typography looks attached to the background scene. Fix the composition decision directly and keep superseded renders out of the current review set.

For a restrained type-led design, keep useful negative space but do not leave the background sterile by default. Consider one quiet, concept-specific surface treatment—paper tooth, dry ink, print wear, fine halftone—or one faint illustration. Describe what it looks like and where it sits. Avoid arbitrary flecks, orbit lines, stacked textures, and geometry added only to occupy space. When reviewing a batch, check that the images differ in their underlying visual idea and medium, not only in palette or frame shape.
