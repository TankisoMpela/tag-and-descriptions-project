# Font Image Director

## Purpose

Turn the font-specific art direction written by Agent 27 into one finished
image at a time. Agent 27 writes a new, detailed prompt for each font from the
font's actual construction and identity. Treat that per-font prompt as the
creative brief. The system does not depend on an old marketplace thumbnail and
does not need one to begin.

Use the attached font specimen or seed image as the authority for the visible
letterforms. Use the exact supplied font name. If a per-font prompt from
`visual-direction/theme.txt` is provided, follow its concept, subject,
composition, medium, palette, texture, lighting, and focus decisions. Do not
replace its art direction with a generic house style or an unrelated reference.
Treat the matching ratio's complete `theme.txt` prompt as the source of truth:
do not silently rewrite it into a new concept or append a new style recipe when
calling the image model. Use only the matching white-background specimen(s) as
image references; do not attach benchmark/research renders unless the seller
specifically asks to use one.

If the seller rejects a direction and asks for a new one, do not improvise a
replacement prompt at render time. First have Agent 27 revise the font's
`theme.txt`, `visual-plan.json`, and `candidate-review.md` with a clearly new
premise that removes the rejected mechanism and follows the research exclusions.
Then generate from that revised prompt and its matching specimen. In particular,
never use an arch/portal title frame or a generic luxury still-life/side-object
composition as a fallback after a rejected direction.

## Shared image rules

- Build each new design around the actual font specimen and the matching
  font-specific brief. Agent 27 derives that brief from the sequentially
  reviewed captions, element-use analyses, and prompt lessons; follow the
  selected font-specific mechanism instead of importing a reference's props,
  palette, or full style.
- For main thumbnails, render the exact font name plus the approved concise
  style descriptor. Use the same descriptor on 3:2 and square main thumbnails;
  keep it title case, secondary, and readable at small size. Preserve the font
  name's supplied capitalization and actual glyph contours; do not force all
  caps or substitute a generic font. Keep important letters fully in frame.
- Give the font name a genuinely large footprint in both ratios: its longest
  line spans at least 75% of frame width and its visible glyph block spans at
  least 35% of frame height, measured without the descriptor. Stack/reflow the
  name or simplify/move the scene when it does not fit; never shrink the type
  to make room for background objects.
- Give the image one visual premise. A type-led poster, a cropped photo, a
  purposeful illustration, or tactile print can work when the font and idea
  support it. Do not fill open space for its own sake.
- Keep the font name as the visual event. Do not reach for glossy luxury
  still-life photography, amber shallow focus, velvet/silk, gold accents, or
  perfume-and-flower props as a generic “premium” treatment. Use photography
  only when its subject is specific to the font idea. Follow Agent 27's
  novelty decision; do not add a repeated house motif.
  Do not revive the seller's rejected defaults: an oversized cropped initial
  with orbit rings, a blue/berry split descriptor panel, or an arch/portal
  title frame. Across a set, vary the actual visual idea and medium, not just
  the palette. A flat type-led image may have one quiet paper/ink/print texture
  or a faint relevant illustration; keep it purposeful and secondary rather
  than leaving a sterile field or adding random marks.
- Keep all title and descriptor lettering as crisp, flat, front-facing graphic
  overlays. Never print, paint, mount, emboss, extrude, or perspective-warp the
  words onto a photographed or rendered object, clothing, page, wall, product,
  or 3D environment. Background photography and 3D scenes may remain behind the
  typography; the words stay independent of their perspective, lighting,
  shadows, and materials.
- In a photo with a model/person, keep the person behind the typography. Crop
  out the eyes/face, show a lower face or body, or blur and subdue the face.
  Never make beauty detail or expression the focal point. Keep the font name
  crisp, readable and more prominent.
- Choose a clear local value contrast where every letter crosses the image.
  Avoid dark-on-dark or similar-value color pairs. Use only the material,
  texture and blur that give the concept its identity; do not stack effects.
- In each direction, choose an intentional, concept-led palette and record the
  title, descriptor, and text-field colors by name and hex. Give the lettering
  color a reason tied to the concept; avoid weak washed-out colors or default
  black/gray. Keep both lines over their named field and check actual contrast.
- Keep one visual premise and only the few background shapes or subjects that
  make it clear. Do not pile up props, labels, textures, or effects to fill
  space. The lettering color and background should feel designed together.
- Before choosing a direction, read `prompt-findings.md` and `prototypes/review.md`
  in `font-factory/Style-References/thumbnail-prompt-research-2026-10-20/`.
  Their written synthesis is sufficient for an ordinary run. Do not reopen
  individual reference or prototype images, which can anchor the result to
  their exact layouts and props. Record the preference principle used and
  what the direction applies or avoids. Inspect a specific example only at
  the seller's request or to resolve an unclear finding.
- Never add glitter, sparkles, starbursts, confetti, floating particles, fake
  lens flare, generic AI ornaments, arbitrary badges, gratuitous glow or
  excessive microtexture. Do not add unrelated props, invented claims, gibberish
  or accidental text. Preserve useful negative space, but give a flat field a
  specific tactile or illustrative treatment when it needs one; never add
  decoration only to fill space.

## Main thumbnail

Create one 3:2 main marketplace thumbnail from scratch. Use either the primary
3:2 direction or the alternate 3:2 direction in the supplied per-font brief.
When making a 1:1 companion, use the same direction. Include the exact font
name and approved descriptor as the only visible copy. Make the design specific
to this font rather than a reusable category template. Do not carry over an old
seller's name, layout, palette, copy, or background.

## Alternate thumbnail direction

The per-font brief contains a primary and a distinctly different alternate
direction, each with 3:2 and 1:1 prompts. Generate the requested ratio from one
chosen direction; when making a thumbnail pair, use the same direction for
both ratios. The alternate must follow its own subject and composition rather
than borrowing the primary direction's artwork or merely changing its palette.
Recompose the square independently. Keep the exact font name and approved
descriptor.

## Supporting image

When the user asks for one supporting image, use the exact phrase they supplied
or approved and the font seed as the letterform authority. Build a new context
around that phrase and font. Do not introduce the font name unless requested.
Use a distinct composition that shows a plausible use of the font, and follow
any supplied supporting-image brief. Add no secondary copy by default.

## Pinterest image

When the user asks for a Pinterest image, use the approved main thumbnail as
identity reference and the exact font seed/name for lettering fidelity. Create
one dedicated 2:3 vertical composition, reworking the scale and placement for
the tall frame rather than stretching the landscape image. Preserve only the
approved copy unless the user requests more.

## Output

Generate exactly one finished image in the requested mode and ratio. Save it
with the filename supplied by the user or workflow. Before returning it, check
that the name is spelled and capitalized correctly, the important glyphs are
unclipped, the composition matches the per-font brief, and the design reads at
small size. This is an acceptance gate, not a description of defects to pass
through. If any check below fails, reject the render, revise the composition or
prompt that caused the failure, and generate a replacement. Repeat until the
image passes; never label a failed render complete or leave it as the selected
output.

### Research-alignment acceptance gate

Compare the actual image with the written seller research and the font-specific
direction. Accept only when all of these are true:

1. The font name is the first read. Its longest line occupies at least 75% of
   the frame width and the name's glyph block occupies at least 35% of frame
   height. Important glyphs are fully visible and preserve the specimen's
   spelling, natural case, contours, joins, spacing, and filled/outlined form.
2. The image communicates one specific idea earned by this font's letterforms,
   name, or buyer context. A subject or material explains that idea or balances
   the title; it does not become a competing hero. Reject generic category
   imagery that could be reused unchanged for an unrelated font.
3. The title is a crisp, flat, front-facing overlay, independent of scene
   perspective, lighting, shadows, and surfaces. It is never lettering printed
   on a photographed object, clothing, page, wall, product, or environment.
4. Every glyph has clear local value contrast against the area behind it. The
   lettering hue and strength belong to the concept and separate from the
   background; reject muddy, washed-out, low-contrast, or arbitrarily bright
   color choices. Check the full title and descriptor at small preview size.
5. The frame feels composed: the title relates deliberately to the crop, scene,
   color field, scale, or one meaningful graphic element. Reject both a blank,
   centered specimen-like field and decorative clutter. Every visible element
   must support the same premise.
6. The image has a memorable visual anchor beyond the lettering. A fine-line
   grid, isolated circle, a few abstract marks, or a solid field by themselves
   do not make a finished concept unless they clearly form a specific, readable
   metaphor. Reject a sparse diagram that leaves most of the frame inert. Use
   one substantial, font-relevant subject, illustration, scene, or clearly
   authored type relationship to make the idea visually immediate; keep it
   subordinate to the name.
7. The palette has visual energy as well as numeric contrast. Give the idea a
   distinctive, concept-led color relationship and a clear accent. Do not rely
   on pale blue lines, faint accents, low-saturation neutrals, or near-monochrome
   color alone to make a flat field feel designed. The title color must feel
   chosen with the background, not merely readable on it. Strong color does not
   mean arbitrary neon or several competing hues.
8. The result does not fall into the research's rejected defaults: generic
   glossy luxury still life or warm hazy side-object/open-title split; textile,
   portrait, or props overwhelming the type; arbitrary geometry; or filler
   effects such as sparkles, particles, starbursts, fake flares, badges, or
   stacked textures. These are not categorical bans when the font-specific
   direction genuinely requires an element, but a palette or prop swap alone
   never justifies repeating a rejected composition.
9. At 300×200 (and a similarly small square preview for 1:1), the name remains
   the immediate read, every letter stays distinguishable, and the image still
   has one coherent premise and a visible focal idea. If it feels empty or its
   color collapses, restructure the image or strengthen the palette; do not
   merely claim the prompt was followed.

Use the per-font concept's own palette and medium. The research is a quality
standard, not a fixed palette or a prop recipe. Treat numeric contrast as only
one color check; visual energy and title/background color harmony are checked
separately. After a failed render, change
the actual cause (title scale, local contrast, crop, competing subject,
generic premise, or clutter) rather than making a cosmetic palette-only edit.
Only the passing image is the final output. Report the output filename and any
remaining visible lettering problem; if no render passes, report that clearly
instead of claiming completion.
